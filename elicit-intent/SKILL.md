# Elicit intent

## Goal

Understand what the person wants well enough to predict their choice on a case
they have not seen, using as little of their time as possible.

## What matters, in order

1. **People recognize what they want far better than they can describe it.**
   Give them concrete things to react to: two short versions, a real past case,
   a sample of the hard part. Asking someone for their rule gets you a guess at
   what sounds right.

2. **The person owns the standard. You own the wording.** Say back what they
   seem to want, with their reason attached, and let them correct it. Never
   fill a gap with your own taste and present it as theirs.

3. **A principle only counts if it changes a decision.** "More rigorous" or
   "more professional" fits many different results. Treat a word like that as
   the start of the conversation. Get a real case where the quality was
   missing, or show short contrasting samples of what the word could mean and
   ask which is closer.

4. **Where a principle stops applying is worth more than the principle.** When
   the person is setting a standard to reuse, check its edges: change one
   detail (no ask this week, bad news, a different reader) and say what you
   would do. Skip this for one-off work.

5. **Their time is the cost.** Make every reply cheap to answer. State your
   own prediction as the default, so they can say "yes", "B", or "go". Tell
   apart facts only they know (which file is current) from taste (which
   layout is better): ask for facts plainly, and show options for taste. Stop
   as soon as more answers would not change what you do.

## Sizing the work to the risk

- Clear request, or cheap to redo: do the work. A set of varied options is
  itself something to react to, so no questions are needed first.
- Costly to redo, hard to undo, or going out to many people: do not build the
  whole thing on a guess. Build the smallest piece that shows the choice
  (one section, one sample line, two folder trees) and get a reaction first.
- The person says to just go, or stops answering: make the call, do the work,
  and say in one line which way you went so they can redirect.

## Check

You understand them when you can predict their pick on a new case. Make the
prediction, show it, and let them confirm or correct. A wrong prediction is a
new example, so keep it.

## What to keep

When the standard will be reused (a saved preference, a guideline, a skill, an
eval case), keep these in the person's own words: the principle with its
reason, the condition where it flips, and one example on each side of that
condition.
